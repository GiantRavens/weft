# For speakers: reviews and recordings

Weft publishes historical texts with the words explained one by one and a guide to how they sound. Some of these texts are in
the languages of living communities, among them the Constitution of the Cherokee Nation of 1827. The word-by-word explanations
on those pages were drafted by an outsider, with help from an AI model, and they stay marked as drafts until a fluent speaker
has reviewed them. This page sets out what Weft asks of a speaker who agrees to help, and what Weft promises in return. Weft
publishes nothing a speaker sends it by email or through the recording page until the speaker has agreed to it in writing. The
GitHub forms linked from each page are different: anything posted there is public at once.

## What Weft asks

- **A review of the words.** Each word on the page has a dictionary form, a grammatical description and an English gloss.
  A speaker can say which are wrong, which are uncertain, and what a word means where the page does not know. On the Cherokee
  Constitution about seventy words have no dictionary form yet (five in the preamble, the rest in Article I); those are the
  ones where a speaker's knowledge matters most.
- **A reading aloud, if the speaker is willing.** The text read line by line, in the speaker's own way of speaking, and a short
  list of single words said twice each, for the words in doubt, with the speaker's own sense of each if they wish to give it. Connected speech carries tone, vowel length and the way words
  run together, which the old writing does not show, so the line is the main unit and single words are only for checking.

## How a recording can be made

The simplest way is a conversation: a call or a meeting with Skip Levens, who runs Weft, with a decent microphone. Weft divides
the recording into lines afterwards. For a speaker who prefers to record alone, each page of this kind has a recording page that
shows one line at a time in the syllabary only, without the romanization or the sound guide, so that the reading is the speaker's
own. It records each line, lets the speaker listen and record again, and saves all the files at the end in one download for the
speaker to send.

## What is published, and where

- Weft's pages do not play audio yet. When they do, a recording will be published on the page for its text, at
  weftlibrary.org, beside the line it reads. Its files go into the public Weft repository on GitHub only if the speaker chooses
  an open license; with all rights kept by the speaker, they are kept out of it.
- A recording is labeled as a modern reading by a fluent speaker in a named dialect (Oklahoma, Kituwah, or as the speaker
  describes it). It is never presented as Weft's reconstruction of how the text sounded in 1828; the page keeps the two apart.
- Corrections to the words are credited on the page as the speaker asks, and they take precedence over Weft's draft.

## The speaker's choices

- **License.** The speaker decides. The choices are: keep all rights, and allow Weft to display the recording by permission
  only; CC BY-SA 4.0, which lets others reuse it with credit under the same terms; or CC0, which places it in the public domain.
  Keeping all rights is the default.
- **Credit.** By name, by a name the speaker chooses, or anonymous.
- **Withdrawal.** The speaker can withdraw a recording or a correction at any time by writing to Weft. A withdrawn recording
  is removed from the page and from Weft's files from then on, and the page says only that a reading was withdrawn. An open
  license cannot be taken back once granted, and copies already made under it, including the repository's history, remain;
  this is a second reason keeping all rights is the default.
- **No AI training.** Weft does not use a speaker's recordings or corrections to train any AI model, and it states this
  condition beside each recording. Weft cannot enforce the condition on others who copy a published page, which is one reason
  keeping all rights is the default. Weft's drafts are written with an AI model's help; a speaker who prefers that their
  corrections not be handled by an AI model at all can say so, and they will be entered by hand.
- **Community labels.** A speaker may ask for Local Contexts Traditional Knowledge or Biocultural labels on their recording,
  and Weft will show them.

## The Cherokee Nation's policy

The Cherokee Nation's policy on artificial intelligence, signed on 21 August 2025, governs the Nation's own government and
businesses and, as reported, requires fluent speakers' input on Cherokee language applications. Weft is not a Cherokee Nation
project and does not claim the Nation's endorsement. It follows the same principle for its own Cherokee pages: the drafts stay
drafts until a speaker has reviewed them.

## How to reach Weft

Write to speakers@weftlibrary.org. A speaker does not need a GitHub account or any other account. Corrections can also be sent
through the forms linked from each page, for those who use GitHub.
